# Content Writing Structures

This guide is about **how to write** events, battles, characters and races. For data fields, loaders and validation, see [`CONTENT_SYSTEMS_GUIDE.md`](CONTENT_SYSTEMS_GUIDE.md). For story-like What If articles, see [`STORY_FORMAT_GUIDE.md`](STORY_FORMAT_GUIDE.md).

The goal is to stop entries from reading like interchangeable encyclopedia filler. Every piece should have a point of view, a concrete anchor, a turn or tension, and a consequence.

## The shared writing spine

Use this five-part spine for any substantial entry:

```text
1. Hook       Begin with a specific image, action, contradiction or question.
2. Context    Give only the history needed to understand the present moment.
3. Pressure   Show what is at stake and who wants incompatible things.
4. Turn       Change the reader's understanding or the available choices.
5. Consequence End with what is different now and who carries the cost.
```

Do not open with a dictionary definition unless the definition itself is contested in-world. Introduce the subject doing something: a commander choosing a retreat, a clerk changing a date, a character refusing an order, or a community preserving a ritual.

### Paragraph rules

- Prefer one concrete anchor per paragraph: an object, sound, gesture, place, document, wound, meal or tool.
- Show the action before explaining its meaning.
- Give important claims a source, speaker or observable example.
- Use short paragraphs for turns, discoveries and emotional reversals.
- Avoid stacking adjectives where one precise noun or verb would do.
- End sections on a changed condition, not a summary of what the section just said.

## 1. Event writing structure

An event records change. Write it as a **before / pressure / decision / after** sequence, not as a date followed by lore.

### Short event: 250–450 words

```text
Title / date / location

Hook: the first visible sign that something is happening.
Before: what was normal, expected or believed immediately beforehand.
Pressure: the people or factions with conflicting aims.
Decision: the action that makes this event different from a report.
After: the immediate result and the first unresolved consequence.
Archive note: source, confidence, canon status or disputed detail.
```

### Major event: 700–1,200 words

Use 3–5 sections:

1. **The approach** — place, conditions and the problem before anyone names it.
2. **The collision** — dialogue, action and competing objectives.
3. **The decision** — the point where somebody chooses and cannot fully undo it.
4. **The first bill** — who benefits, who pays and what system changes.
5. **The record** — what later accounts get wrong or leave out.

An event should answer: *Why today? Why these people? Why can this not simply be filed as ordinary?*

## 2. Battle writing structure

A battle is not a catalogue of units. It is a chain of decisions under pressure. Write the battle through **objectives and changing information**, not through a neutral camera floating above the field.

### Battle article: 900–1,500 words

```text
1. Situation       What each side believes before contact.
2. Ground          Weather, terrain, routes, visibility and civilian presence.
3. Opening move    The first tactic and the assumption behind it.
4. Friction       The unexpected fact: delay, betrayal, terrain, morale or supply.
5. Decision point  The command or refusal that determines the shape of the fight.
6. Break           Retreat, surrender, breakthrough, evacuation or stalemate.
7. Aftermath       Tactical result, strategic result, human cost and next conflict.
```

Write at least one scene at ground level. Name what a soldier can hear, carry, see or misunderstand. Then widen the lens to the map and campaign. The reader should understand both **what it felt like** and **why it mattered**.

Do not make victory mean that every objective succeeded. A side can hold the field and lose its alliance, supplies, legitimacy or commander. State the objective that was achieved and the objective that failed.

## 3. Character writing structure

A character article should reveal a person through **want, method, contradiction and cost**. Do not write a résumé of everything they have ever done.

### Character profile: 500–900 words

```text
1. Entrance        A specific action that shows the character at work.
2. Want            What they are pursuing now, not their generic ambition.
3. Method          How they normally get it; include a habit or signature.
4. Pressure       The relationship, fear, duty or fact they cannot control.
5. Contradiction   The principle they claim versus the choice they make.
6. Cost            What their method has already damaged or will endanger.
7. Open question   What the player can still change, discover or challenge.
```

Give the character one concrete object or repeated action: a ledger they annotate, a helmet they refuses to replace, a cup left untouched, a route memorised, a door they never use. Let it recur with changed meaning.

A good character entry includes at least one moment where they are competent, one where they are wrong, and one where their limitation matters. Avoid describing a character as “complex”; dramatise the conflict that makes them complex.

### Relationship paragraph

For each important relationship, write:

```text
They believe ___ about the other person.
They need ___ from them.
They will not forgive ___ yet.
The last event changed ___ because ___ .
```

This keeps relations dynamic instead of reducing them to “ally,” “enemy” or a numerical score.

## 4. Race and culture writing structure

A race article describes a living population, not a personality template. Start with a **community in a place**, then show how history, environment and institutions shape different lives within it.

### Race overview: 800–1,400 words

```text
1. Self-description   What they call themselves and what outsiders get wrong.
2. Daily life          Work, family, food, movement, housing and time.
3. History             One formative event and the unresolved consequence.
4. Institutions        Authority, law, religion, trade, kinship or education.
5. Internal voices     At least three groups, regions, classes or generations.
6. Contact             A relationship with another people, including its cause.
7. Present pressure    The current problem creating choices and disagreement.
8. Future question     What can change, and who benefits or loses if it does?
```

### Required balance

For every broad cultural claim, include a counterexample or a boundary:

- “Many communities value X; the border settlements reject it because Y.”
- “The tradition is old, but younger members are changing it through Z.”
- “Outsiders call this practice A; practitioners describe it as B.”

Separate biological traits, cultural practices, political positions and individual character. Do not make a race inherently good, evil, clever, loyal, primitive or unified. Differences in morality and politics belong to people and institutions.

### Race scene

Every major race entry should include one short scene or vignette—such as a meal, council, funeral, apprenticeship, market or border crossing. The scene is where the reader learns what the overview claims. Make at least two members disagree about the meaning of the same custom.

## Tone and point of view

Choose the document's voice before drafting:

- **Archive:** restrained, sourced, explicit about uncertainty.
- **Eyewitness:** sensory, limited, personal and openly fallible.
- **Military report:** objective, operational, with omissions made visible.
- **Character account:** biased, intimate and shaped by what the speaker wants remembered.
- **Cultural account:** specific, respectful and clear about who is speaking.

Do not mix voices accidentally. If an archive summary contains a rumour, label it. If a character's account is biased, let the bias appear in selection and wording rather than adding an authorial disclaimer to every paragraph.

## Final writing test

Before converting prose into data, ask:

```text
□ Is the opening specific enough to picture?
□ Does someone want something in the piece?
□ Is there pressure or disagreement rather than only information?
□ Does a decision or discovery change the direction?
□ Is at least one consequence visible in a person or place?
□ Does the ending leave a live question, cost or next hook?
□ Could this entry be swapped onto another subject without changing much?
   If yes, it is still generic—add a distinctive scene, voice or contradiction.
```
